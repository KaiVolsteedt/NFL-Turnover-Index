Where the swing comes from

In win-probability points. The three parts add up to the swing.

How the number is built

Every turnover runs through the same five steps. The grey boxes show the live values for the situation above.

  1. 1

    Read the moment

    Quarter and clock, score margin, down and distance, and field position describe the play the offense was about to run.

  2. 2

    Value the ball before

    Expected points (EP) say what a drive from this spot is worth: about 0.4 from the offense's own 20 and about 4.8 from the opponent's 10. Down and distance adjust it. On 4th down it uses the odds of converting.

  3. 3

    Value the ball after

    The same table from where the defense takes over, after any return. A return touchdown counts as seven points, and the offense then receives the kickoff.

  4. 4

    Turn points into win probability

    A bell curve whose spread narrows as the clock runs down, so a point late is worth far more than a point early. A team that leads and holds the ball late also gets credit for running the clock.

  5. 5

    Subtract and label

    The swing is win probability before minus win probability after, on a scale from 0 to 100. It lands in one of five labels.

The formulas

weight         w(t)   = min(1, sqrt(t / 120))
effective      E      = margin + EP x w(t)
spread         s(t)   = sqrt(13.5^2 x t/3600
                             + 5.5^2 x (1 - e^(-t/120))
                             + 0.5^2)
win prob       WP     = Phi(E / s(t))
swing          Swing  = WP before - WP after

t = seconds left in the game
EP = expected points, offense positive
Phi = standard normal curve

The five labels

    The bar on the card stretches the low end so small swings stay readable.

    This prototype uses hand-tuned constants so it runs entirely in the page. A production version would replace the expected-points table and the spread with models fit to play-by-play data, such as the free nflfastR dataset, and could add a final swing that updates once the opponent's drive ends.
